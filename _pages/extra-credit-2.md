## Introduction

On October 7, 2026, I attended an online workshop called *Breaking Up with Google Docs*, organized by NYU New York Data Services. The workshop took place from 6 PM to 6:30 PM UAE time and introduced us to two note-taking applications, Joplin and Obsidian, as alternatives to platforms like Google Docs, Microsoft Word, and Evernote.

I was interested in attending because I use Google Docs quite often for my university assignments, especially when taking notes, writing essays, or working on group projects. I had heard of Obsidian before from my classmates but never really explored how it worked, and I wasn't familiar with Joplin at all. I was curious about what made these applications different from the ones I already use and whether they would actually be useful for my studies.

The workshop mainly focused on privacy, organizing information, and having more control over our own files. We also had the chance to see demonstrations of both applications and how they approach note-taking differently.

## Why Look Beyond Google Docs?

The workshop began by introducing the idea of using privacy-focused alternatives to traditional writing and note-taking platforms. Most of us are probably used to storing our work on cloud-based services like Google Docs without really thinking about where our information is being stored or how much control we have over it.

One of the main points discussed was the importance of data ownership. Instead of relying entirely on a company's cloud storage, applications like Joplin and Obsidian allow users to keep their notes locally on their own devices. This means we can have more control over where our files are stored and how we manage them.

I thought this was interesting because I usually choose applications based on how convenient they are rather than thinking about privacy or long-term access. The workshop made me realize that the way we store our work can be just as important as how we create it.

## Getting Familiar with Joplin

The first application we explored was Joplin, an open-source note-taking application that allows users to create and organize notes into notebooks. During the demonstration i saw how notes could be grouped into different categories, making it easier to manage information for different projects or subjects.

One feature I found useful was how Joplin supports Markdown, which allows users to format text using simple symbols rather than relying entirely on traditional formatting buttons. It also supports attachments, checklists, and synchronization options.

I liked how straightforward the interface looked. It reminded me of a more organized version of a regular note-taking app where everything could be separated into notebooks. I could imagine using something like this to keep my notes from different university classes in one place without having everything mixed together.

## Exploring Obsidian

We then moved on to Obsidian, which approaches note-taking a little differently. While Joplin focuses more on organizing notes into notebooks, Obsidian allows users to create connections between different notes through internal links.

One of the most interesting things about Obsidian was how it stores notes as Markdown files. This means that the notes are not limited to being opened in one specific application, since Markdown files can also be edited using other text editors.

We also saw how Obsidian organizes notes through folders and properties, allowing users to create their own system for managing information. I thought the idea of connecting notes together was interesting because sometimes information from one class can relate to something we learned in another, but we don't always think to make those connections.

Compared to Joplin, Obsidian seemed to offer more flexibility in how someone could build their own note-taking system, although I could also imagine it taking longer to get used to.

## Comparing Joplin and Obsidian

Another part of the workshop involved comparing the strengths of both applications. Although they share some similarities, such as local storage and Markdown support, they are designed for slightly different workflows.

Joplin seemed more suitable for someone who wants a straightforward way to organize notes into notebooks, while Obsidian seemed more focused on building connections between ideas and creating a larger personal knowledge system.

We also learned about differences in encryption, plugins, synchronization, and customization. One important distinction is that Joplin is open-source software, while Obsidian is proprietary, even though it uses Markdown files and allows users to store their notes locally.

I appreciated that the workshop didn't present one application as automatically better than the other. Instead, it showed how choosing a tool depends on what we need it for and how we prefer to organize our work.

## Connecting the Workshop to Digital Humanities

One of the biggest connections to our class I made during this workshop was that we have already been using Markdown and GitHub Pages to create and publish our assignments.

Before taking this class I wasn't really familiar with Markdown, but now we use it to format text, insert images, and embed links into our websites. When I saw that both Joplin and Obsidian support Markdown, I immediately recognized some of the formatting techniques we had already been practicing.

I also thought about how we have been organizing our GitHub repositories into folders and files. In a way, this is similar to how Joplin and Obsidian allow users to organize information into different notebooks or folders.

Another connection was the idea of having more control over our digital work. In Digital Humanities, we are learning how information is structured and presented. This workshop made me think more about the tools I use every day and how they might influence the way I organize and access information.

## Final Reflection and Future Uses

Overall, I enjoyed learning about Joplin and Obsidian because they introduced me to alternatives I probably wouldn't have explored on my own. Before the workshop, I mainly thought of note-taking applications as places to write and save information, but I hadn't really considered how their different features could change the way we study or organize ideas.

As an IM student, I think Obsidian could be especially useful for organizing research and references for future projects. I often find myself working on different assignments that involve coding, design, and storytelling, so having a way to connect related notes could be helpful.

I could also imagine using one of these applications for a future capstone project, especially when gathering research and keeping track of ideas over a longer period of time. Although I don't think I would completely stop using Google Docs just because its very habitual for me and I have been using it since I was in 5th grade, particularly for group assignments, but I would be interested in trying Obsidian for my own notes and individual projects.
