<h1>Gemini</h1>

<p>
As a nineteen-year-old university student majoring in Interactive Media with minors in Film and New Media and Design, my relationship with technology is less about learning tools and more about navigating an environment I have always inhabited. For my generation, digital literacy is often framed as an innate fluency, but moving through an academic space has revealed that being a digital native does not automatically make one digitally literate. There is a vast divide between consuming digital culture and critically dissecting, manipulating, or building the systems that power it.
</p>

<p>
In my day-to-day and academic routine, content creation and social interaction are second nature. Design workflows rely heavily on the Adobe Creative Cloud, Photoshop, Illustrator, and Premiere Pro, alongside Figma for prototyping user interfaces and spatial layouts. On the interactive media side, code serves as a primary creative medium; standard web technologies like HTML, CSS, and JavaScript interface with creative coding libraries like p5.js and Three.js to construct responsive visual environments. When managing research data and organizing creative projects, workflows depend on tools like Notion or Obsidian for networked note-taking, combined with Zotero for managing academic citations. For data manipulation, analysis, and version control, tools range from basic spreadsheets and Python, using Pandas for dataset analysis, to GitHub for managing software repositories and tracking iterative changes. Stepping into an interdisciplinary classroom, however, immediately reshapes this perspective. The range of skill sets in the room is striking. A Computer Science major approaches a project through code efficiency, algorithm optimization, and robust backend logic. An Environmental Studies or Engineering student views data through environmental sensors, spatial mapping, or physical constraints. A Literature or Creative Writing major brings a deep sensitivity to narrative structure, rhetoric, and textual nuance. Coming from Interactive Media, Film, and Design, my focus sits at the intersection of aesthetics, human-computer interaction, and visual communication.
</p>

<p>
This mix of backgrounds highlights that computational thinking is not a single, uniform skill owned by computer scientists. Instead, it is a flexible framework that evolves through cross-disciplinary collaboration. Working alongside a programmer teaches me how to deconstruct a complex problem into modular components; collaborating with a writer challenges me to think about how interactive mechanics themselves convey narrative meaning. These exchanges push my computational thinking beyond mere syntax toward conceptual problem-solving, asking not just how to code an interaction, but why that interaction matters humanistically. This is where the Digital Humanities offer exciting new horizons. Rather than viewing technology strictly as a set of technical utilities, Digital Humanities bridges computational methods with critical cultural inquiry. It demonstrates how data analysis, visualization, and generative frameworks can be used to interrogate history, media, and human behavior. Learning to see datasets not as objective truths, but as curated, culturally situated artifacts changes how I approach design. It asks us to look critically at what is included, what is omitted, and how algorithmic structures enforce specific worldviews.
</p>

<p>
Reflecting on my current abilities, I feel confident in visual composition, UI/UX prototyping, and creative coding. Yet, I am discovering that my technical understanding often hits a wall when moving from front-end user experience to deep data analysis and backend systems. Emerging tools and AI paradigms are reshaping this dynamic entirely. Machine learning models are no longer just abstract concepts; they are becoming daily creative collaborators and research assistants. Discovering how to prompt, fine-tune, and critically evaluate AI outputs has made me realize that the future of design lies in understanding how data shapes machine intelligence.
</p>

<p>
Looking ahead, I want to explore ways of thinking that treat AI not as a shortcut, but as a medium to be critiqued and repurposed. I am eager to delve deeper into ethics in data visualization, algorithmic bias, and physical computing, exploring how code escapes the screen to interact with tangible space. True digital literacy requires moving beyond operational skill to cultivate a critical, creative, and ethical orientation toward technology. By combining interactive design, media theory, and computational inquiry, I hope to build digital experiences that are not only functional and visually engaging, but deeply reflective of the human context behind the data.
</p>

<h1>Claude</h1>

<p>
I came into this course assuming "digital literacy" meant something close to what I already had. I've been editing video since I was fourteen, I can find my way around Figma and Premiere without thinking about it, and I post across three or four platforms without much friction. What this class has actually shown me is that fluency with tools is only the surface layer. Real digital literacy is about understanding the systems underneath the tools: how platforms shape what gets seen, how data gets collected and structured, how algorithms make decisions I don't get to watch happen, and how all of that intersects with the stories I'm trying to tell as someone majoring in Interactive Media with minors in Film and New Media and Design.
</p>


<p>
A lot of the strategies this course names explicitly, I've been doing intuitively for years without a vocabulary for them. I already think about aspect ratio and platform-native formatting when I cut a video for TikTok versus a festival submission. I already know how metadata, captions, and alt text change discoverability, even if I hadn't framed that as "structuring information for a system to parse." Version control instincts from group film shoots, naming conventions, shared drives, and shot logs, turn out to be a lightweight form of the same discipline computer science students learn through Git. Recognizing that overlap has been one of the more useful moments of the course: I wasn't starting from zero, I was starting from a different discipline's version of the same skill.
</p>


<p>
This is where the gaps show up. I use Notion and Google Workspace to organize project research, and I've dabbled in Airtable for tracking assets across a production, but I had never touched anything like OpenRefine, Zotero for structured citation management, or basic Python/pandas for cleaning a dataset before this class. Being handed a messy CSV and asked to find a pattern in it was uncomfortable in a useful way. It's a completely different kind of attention than editing a timeline. Tableau and Datawrapper for visualization felt closer to home, since they're really design problems: what story does this chart tell, and how do I keep it honest while making it legible. That's the throughline I keep noticing: data visualization is just information design with a stricter set of constraints.
</p>

<p>
What's struck me most is how differently everyone in this class approaches the same assignment. The Environmental Studies students think in terms of longitudinal datasets and uncertainty; the Engineering students want to build something that scales and breaks the problem into reproducible steps; the Literature and Creative Writing people are far more attentive to voice, ambiguity, and what a dataset leaves out than I initially was. Watching a CS major approach a "creative" prompt as an optimization problem, and watching a writer push back on what counts as "clean" data, has done more to expand my sense of computational thinking than any tutorial could. Computational thinking isn't just decomposition and abstraction in the textbook sense. It's watching people from different fields decompose the same problem differently and realizing my own default approach is just one option among several.
</p>

<p>
Before this course, I thought of "the humanities" and "computation" as running on separate tracks that occasionally borrowed from each other. Seeing text-mining applied to literary corpora, or network analysis applied to historical archives, reframed data analysis for me as an interpretive act rather than a purely technical one, much closer to editing or curation than I expected. That reframing matters for my own work: a lot of interactive media is essentially applied digital humanities, using structured data and computation to tell a story or build an experience, whether that's a documentary interface or a generative installation.
</p>

<p>
I've used generative AI tools mostly as production shortcuts, rough transcription, first-pass color suggestions, and brainstorming. This course has pushed me to think about them less as neutral tools and more as systems trained on specific data with specific blind spots, which changes how I want to use them: more critically, and more as a collaborator to interrogate than a black box to trust. Going forward, I want to get more comfortable with basic scripting so I'm not dependent on someone else's interface to ask my own questions of a dataset, and I want to keep working alongside people outside my discipline, because the biggest jumps in how I think about this material have come from arguments with people who don't share my assumptions.
</p>
