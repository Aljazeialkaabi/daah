<p>
As a 19 year old Interactive Media student with minors in Film and New Media and Design, I think digital literacy is something I use every day without always thinking of it as a skill. I already know how to use tools like Google Drive, Canva, Adobe programs, p5.js, GitHub, and social media, but I am realizing that knowing how to use a tool is not the same as understanding how it affects the way I create and communicate. For content creation, I am comfortable using digital tools to make visual and interactive work. In my classes, I have been learning p5.js and basic HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, which has made me think differently about what it means to create something digitally. Instead of only making an image or video, I can make something that responds to a person. I also use editing and design software for film and visual projects. Social media has taught me how to communicate quickly and notice how people react to different kinds of content. At the same time, I sometimes use these platforms automatically without thinking about how algorithms influence what I see.
</p>

<p>
Research and data management are areas where I still have a lot to learn. I know how to organize files in Google Drive and use spreadsheets, but I never really thought much about how important the way we organize data actually is. In the reading [Data Organization in Spreadsheets](https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.2017.1375989), Broman and Woo explain how small things like inconsistent naming, leaving cells empty, or putting too much information in one cell can make data harder to work with and lead to errors. I think I used to see spreadsheets as just a place to store information, but this reading made me realize that organizing data properly is just as important as analyzing it. It also made me think about how easily small mistakes can affect the results, especially when working with large amounts of information. I still want to improve my skills in data analysis and visualization, but I think understanding how to organize data correctly is an important starting point.
<p>

<p>
I think having students from different majors in the same class is interesting because everyone has their own way of thinking and approaching things. For example, someone studying Computer Science might focus more on coding and how something works, while someone studying Film and New Media might care more about the visuals and how a story is told. I think bringing these different perspectives together can help us come up with ideas we might not have thought of on our own. It also helps me see that there isn't always one way to solve a problem, and I can learn a lot from how other people approach the same task differently.</p>

<p>
One of the things we've done in Digital Humanities that made me think differently about analyzing information was working with a corpus of science fiction texts. Instead of reading each text individually, we used digital tools to look at patterns in words across different stories and time periods. We explored things like word frequency and KWIC, which helped us see how certain words were being used in different contexts. I think what stood out to me was that just because a word appears a lot doesn't necessarily mean it has the same meaning every time. We still need to look at the context and think about what the results are actually telling us. This made me realize that digital tools can help us find patterns, but they can't do all the interpreting for us.
<p>

<p>
Overall, I think my digital literacy is strongest when I am experimenting and learning by buildings things, but I still have a lot to improve when it comes to data management, analysis, and understanding what happens behind the tools I use. This course is making me realize that digital literacy is knowing how to question technology, understand information, work with people who think differently, and make decisions about how digital tools should be used. I want to explore more ways of combining code, design, film, data, and AI instead of keeping them separate. I think that is where I can develop my own way of thinking as an Interactive Media student.
</p>

<p>
Looking ahead, I want to explore ways of thinking that treat AI not as a shortcut, but as a medium to be critiqued and repurposed. I am eager to delve deeper into ethics in data visualization, algorithmic bias, and physical computing, exploring how code escapes the screen to interact with tangible space. True digital literacy requires moving beyond operational skill to cultivate a critical, creative, and ethical orientation toward technology. By combining interactive design, media theory, and computational inquiry, I hope to build digital experiences that are not only functional and visually engaging, but deeply reflective of the human context behind the data.
</p>


<p>
I came into this course assuming "digital literacy" meant something close to what I already had. I've been editing video since I was fourteen, I can find my way around Figma and Premiere without thinking about it, and I post across three or four platforms without much friction. What this class has actually shown me is that fluency with tools is only the surface layer. Real digital literacy is about understanding the systems underneath the tools: how platforms shape what gets seen, how data gets collected and structured, how algorithms make decisions I don't get to watch happen, and how all of that intersects with the stories I'm trying to tell as someone majoring in Interactive Media with minors in Film and New Media and Design.
</p>


<p>
A lot of the strategies this course names explicitly, I've been doing intuitively for years without a vocabulary for them. I already think about aspect ratio and platform-native formatting when I cut a video for TikTok versus a festival submission. I already know how metadata, captions, and alt text change discoverability, even if I hadn't framed that as "structuring information for a system to parse." Version control instincts from group film shoots, naming conventions, shared drives, and shot logs, turn out to be a lightweight form of the same discipline computer science students learn through Git. Recognizing that overlap has been one of the more useful moments of the course: I wasn't starting from zero, I was starting from a different discipline's version of the same skill.
</p>


<p>
 Being handed a messy CSV and asked to find a pattern in it was uncomfortable in a useful way. It's a completely different kind of attention than editing a timeline. Tableau and Datawrapper for visualization felt closer to home, since they're really design problems: what story does this chart tell, and how do I keep it honest while making it legible. That's the throughline I keep noticing: data visualization is just information design with a stricter set of constraints.
</p>

<p>
What's struck me most is how differently everyone in this class approaches the same assignment. The Environmental Studies students think in terms of longitudinal datasets and uncertainty; the Engineering students want to build something that scales and breaks the problem into reproducible steps; the Literature and Creative Writing people are far more attentive to voice, ambiguity, and what a dataset leaves out than I initially was. Watching a CS major approach a "creative" prompt as an optimization problem, and watching a writer push back on what counts as "clean" data, has done more to expand my sense of computational thinking than any tutorial could. Computational thinking isn't just decomposition and abstraction in the textbook sense. It's watching people from different fields decompose the same problem differently and realizing my own default approach is just one option among several.
</p>

<p>
Before this course, I thought of "the humanities" and "computation" as running on separate tracks that occasionally borrowed from each other. Seeing text-mining applied to literary corpora, or network analysis applied to historical archives, reframed data analysis for me as an interpretive act rather than a purely technical one, much closer to editing or curation than I expected. That reframing matters for my own work: a lot of interactive media is essentially applied digital humanities, using structured data and computation to tell a story or build an experience, whether that's a documentary interface or a generative installation.
</p>

<p>
I've used generative AI tools mostly as production shortcuts, rough transcription, first-pass color suggestions, and brainstorming. This course has pushed me to think about them less as neutral tools and more as systems trained on specific data with specific blind spots, which changes how I want to use them: more critically, and more as a collaborator to interrogate than a black box to trust. Going forward, I want to get more comfortable with basic scripting so I'm not dependent on someone else's interface to ask my own questions of a dataset, and I want to keep working alongside people outside my discipline, because the biggest jumps in how I think about this material have come from arguments with people who don't share my assumptions.
</p>
